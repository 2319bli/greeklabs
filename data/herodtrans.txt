207. Indeed, on the one hand, these men intended to do something in this way; but the Greeks in Thermopylae, since the Persians were near the entrance of the pass, in dread, they began to plan about withdrawal. It seemed a good idea for the other Peloponnesians, having gone to the Peloponnese to keep the isthmus under guard. But Leonidas, with the Phocians and Locrians angered by this plan, voted both to stay there and to send messengers to the cities, ordering them to come to help, as there were too few to ward off the army of the Medes.

208. While they were planning these things, Xerxes was sending a mounted scout to see how many they were and what they were doing. While he was still in Thessaly, he had heard that a small army was assembled there and that its leaders were Spartans and Leonidas of the Heracleid clan.
[2] As the horseman drove up to the camp, he started to observe but could not look down on all the camp, for it was not possible to look down on the men who were arranged inside the wall, which they had under guard, having restored it. He was observing the men outside, whose weapons were lying in front of the wall - and it happened, at that time, the Spartans were posted outside.

[3] Indeed he saw some of the men exercising, and others combing their hair. Watching these things, he was amazed, and observed their large numbers. Having learnt everything exactly, he rode back at his leisure for no one pursued him or paid him much attention.  Having gone away he started to tell Xerxes everything he had seen.

209. On hearing this, Xerxes was not able to work out the reality of the situation, that they were preparing both to perish and to deal death to the best of their ability. But since they seem to be acting ludicrously, he sent for Demartus, son of Ariston, who was in camp.
[2] Xerxes asked him when he had arrived about each of these things, wishing to learn what was being done by the Spartans. He said:
"While you have heard from me before when we were setting out for Greece, about those men, when you heard, you made me a laughing stock for saying how I saw these events would turn out. For it is my priority, O king, to tell the truth in your presence."
[3] Listen now too - these men have arrived to fight us over the pass and they are preparing [with] these things. For their custom is as follows - when they are going to put their life in danger, they pay attention to the heads.
[4] But know, if you subdue both those men and what is left behind in Sparta, there is no other race of men which will raise its head and withstand you, O king. For now you are attacking both the finest royal power and city of those in Greece and the best men."
[5] What he said seemed to be completely unbelievable to Xerxes, and he asked for a second time, in what way they would fight his army. And he [Demartus] said: "O king, consider me a liar, if these things do not turn out in the way in which I say."

210. Saying these things he did not persuade Xerxes. Indeed, he let four days go by, always hoping that they would run away. But on the fifth, since they were not withdrawing but seemed to him to stay through characteristics of utter recklessness and poor judgement, he became angry and sent both Medes and Cissians against them, commanding them to capture them alive and lead them into his presence.
[2] The Medes fell upon the Greeks in a rush, many men were falling but others attacked in turn and they were not driven off, despite suffering great setbacks - they were making it clear to everyone and not least to the king himself that while there were many humans, there were few [real] men. The battle lasted throughout the day.

211. Since the Medes were being roughly handled, then while these withdrew gradually, the Persians, whom the king called Immortals, whom Hydarnes was commanding, took their place and attacked in turn; on the assumption that these men would easily succeed.

[2] And when these men too engaged with the Greeks, they achieved nothing more than the army of the Medes, but the same things, because both fighting in a narrow pass and using spears shorter than the Greeks', they were unable to use this number.

[3] The Spartans were fighting in a manner of esteem, revealing other things - knowing well how to fight amongst those who didn't, and in particular whenever they turned their backs and apparently flee en masse. The barbarians seeing them fleeing, with both shouting and din, would go after them but the Spartans when they were caught, would turn about so they were face to face with the barbarians.
And having turned they would strike down countless Persians in a mass; when this happened few of the Spartiates themselves would fall. But when the Persians were able to seize nothing by making an attempt on the entrance to the pass, both attacking by ranks and in every other way, they retreated.

212. In these attacks of the battle, it is said that the king, while observing, jumped three times from his throne, fearing for his army. At that time they fought in this way but on the next [day] the barbarians contended no better in battle. For they joined battle believing that in as much as they were few, [the Greeks] would be both wounded and not able to raise their hands against them.

[2] The Greeks were marshalled by ranks and nations, and each fought in turn, except the Phocians; these were drawn up up on the mountain to guard the shortcut. When the Persians found nothing different than what they saw on the previous [day], they departed.

213. With the king at a loss in what way to deal with the present circumstance, Epialtes, son of Eurydemus, a Malian man, came to have discussions with him. He, thinking that he would take something great from the king, both revealed the shortcut across the mountain leading to Thermopylae, and destroyed those of the Greeks remaining there.

[2] But later, in fear of the Spartans, he fled to Thessaly, and a price was put on his head while he was in exile by the Pylagoroi, when the Amphictyons were assembled at Thermopylae. Some time later, for he came back from exile to Anticyra, he was killed by a Trachinian man [called] Athenades.

[3] This Athenades killed Epialtes for a different reason, which I will reveal in words later, however no less honoured by the Spartans.

214. Epialtes died later than these events. But there is another account told - that Onetes, son of Phanagoras, a man of Carystus and Corydallus of Anticyra are the ones who said these words to the kings and led the Persians round the mountain - to me at any rate it is no way reliable.

[2] For while it is necessary to make a judgement from this that, of the Greeks, the Pylagori set a price on not Onetes and Corrydallus, but on the Trachinian Epialtes, having in my opinion, acquired the most exact [detail] - but we know that Epialtes was in exile because of this charge.

[3] For while Onetes, even though he was not Malian, might have known this path if he often visited the land, rather it was Epialtes who led them on the path around the mountain - I record this man as responsible.

215. Xerxes, since the things which Epialtes promised to achieve pleased him, at once, having become overjoyed, he sent Hydarnes and those whom Hydarnes commanded. He set off out of the camp at around [the time of] the lighting of the lamps. The native Malians discovered this path, finding it when they led the Thessalians to the Phocians, at that time when the Phocians, having blocked the entrance to the pass with a wall, were protected from the war; indeed since then it was admitted that the path was in no way a good thing for the Malians.

216. And this mountain path is like this; it starts from the Asypus river flowing through the gorge and the same name is used for this mountain and the path - Anopaea. This Anopaea stretches along the spine of the mountain and ends at the city of Alpenus, which is the first [city] of the Locrians in the direction of the Malians and at the stone called "of the black-bottomed" and of the seats of the Cercopes - and there it is at its narrowest.

217. Indeed by this path (as described) the Persians, having crossed the Asypus, marched for the whole night with the Oetaean mountains on this right and the Trachinians [ones] on this left. And indeed, dawn was glowing and they came to the summit of the mountain.

[2] On this part of the mountain, 1000 hoplites of Phocians were keeping guard, as I have shown previously, both protecting their own land and guarding the path. The lower part of the pass was being guarded by those whom I have mentioned but Phocians volunteers, having promised Leonidas, were guarding the path through the mountain.

218. The Phocians learned that they had climbed up in the following way; for the Persians, climbing up, escaped their attention as the whole mountain was covered with oak trees. Indeed, while there was an absence of wind, when a great noise arose like leaves spread underneath feet, the Phocians jumped up and put in their weapons and at once the barbarians were there.

[2] When they saw the men putting on their weapons, they were amazed, for they had met with an army while expecting no one to appear to oppose them: then Hydarnes, terrified that the Phocians might be Spartans, asked Epialtes from what the army came and, having found out exactly, marshalled the Persians for battle.

[3] The Phocians, as they were being struck by many showers of arrows, went off in flight to the summit of the mountain, thinking that the Persians had rushed against them at the start and prepared to be destroyed. Indeed, while these men were thinking these things, the Persians with Epialtes and Hydarnes paid no heed to the Phocians but went down the mountain at top speed.

219. The prophet Megistias, having examined the sacrificial offerings, first revealed to those of the Greeks in Thermopylae that death was going to be upon them at dawn, and then deserters came too who were announcing the flanking manoeuvre of the Persians. These men told them this while it was still night, and thirdly, the day-watchers having run down from the heights by the time that day was starting to appear.

[2] The Greeks then planned and their opinions were split - for some were against abandoning their post but others refused to agree. After this, having parted, some separated and scattered each turning to their own city but others of them prepared to remain right there with Leonidas.

220. It is also said that Leonidas himself sent them away, anxious that they would be destroyed [but he considered] it was not honourable for him and the Spartiates who were present to leave the battle-line to which they had come to guard it, in the beginning.

[2] And I am most strongly inclined to this view - that Leonidas, when he realised that the allies were unenthusiastic and not willing to share the danger, ordered them to depart - but that it was not right for him to go away. Great renown would be left for him if he stayed right there and the good fortune of Sparta would not be wiped out.

[3] To the Spartiates enquiring about this war at once at the first moment it broke out, the response had been given by the Pythia that either Sparta would be ruined by the barbarians or their king would be killed. She responded in words in hexameters saying the following.

[4] For you, inhabitants of spacious Sparta, either a great, glorious city will be destroyed by brave descendents of Perseus or, if not that, the boundary of Lacedaemon will mourn a dead king from the line of Heracles. For the might of bulls nor lions will not restrain him in violent hostility, for he has the might of Zeus. I say that he will not be restrained before he divides entirely up one of the two. Considering this and wishing to lay up a store of glory for the Spartans alone, Leonidas sent away the allies rather than them leaving in disorder because of a difference in opinion.

221. And this is not the least evidence I have about this [matter] - that Leonidas clearly, in order that he didn't die along with them, sent away the prophet, the Acarnanian, said to be descended from Melampuos, Megistias who was following this army and who told him what would turn out to happen for them from the sacrifices. But he, being sent away himself, did not leave but sent away the child who was on campaign with him and was the only one born to him.

222. Some of the dismissed allies left in order both to go away and obey Leonidas but the Thespians and Thebans remained alone beside the Spartans. And of these men, the Thebans were remaining reluctantly and not wanting to. For Leonidas was keeping them back, treating them as hostages.  The Thespians were very willing - they said that they would not depart, having abandoned Leonidas and those with him, but would die alongside him, having remained. Demophilus, son of Diadromes, was in command of them.

223. With the sun having set, when Xerxes made his libations, pausing for a time until what was roughly the busiest time of the market, made his attack - for the instruction was given to do this by Epialtes, for the descent from the mountain is more direct and the ground to cover is much shorter than either the flanking or the ascent.

[2] Indeed the barbarians, accompanying Xerxes attacked and the Greeks around Leonidas, knowing that they were marking their advance with the prospect of death, now went out much further than before into the broader part of the pass. For the barricade of the wall was being guarded in previous days, withdrawing to the narrows, they were fighting there. 

[3] And then/now, engaging outside of the narrows, many of the barbarians were falling in a mass - for the leaders of the ranks, holding whips, behind them were striking everyone, always them onwards. Indeed many of them were falling into the sea and dying but still many more were trampled alive by the others - no notice was taken of the people being killed. 

[4] For in as much as the Greeks knew that death was about to come upon them, brought about by those coming round the mountain, they displayed the most intense strength they had against the barbarians - being reckless and without thought of life.

224. Now the spears for the majority of the men already happened to be broken but the men were killing the Persians with swords.  And/even Leonidas, becoming the bravest man fell in this struggle and the other distinguished men of the Spartan with him, whose names I learnt as they were of worthy men - and indeed I have learnt [the names] of all 300. 

[2] And indeed many others of the Persians - renowned ones at that - fell there - and indeed among them two sons of Darius.

225. Indeed two brothers of Xerxes fell fighting there and there was much jostling over the body of Leonidas by both the Persians and the Spartans, until the Greeks, in their valour, dragged it away and sent their enemy running four times. This combat continued until the men with Epialtes appeared.

[2] When the Greeks learnt that these men had come from then on the contest finally turned. For they withdrew back to the narrow part of the pass and, having gone past the wall, they went and took their position on the hill, and close-packed except the Thebans. The hill is in the entrance of the pass where the stone lion in honour of Leonidas now stands.

[3] In this place, defending themselves with daggers if they still happened to have them and with hands and mouths, the barbarians buried them by throwing missiles - some attacking from opposite them and demolishing the defensive wall and others going around them from all sides all around.

226. While the Spartans and Thespians conducted themselves in such a way, nevertheless the man who acquitted himself best is said to be Diecenes, the Spartiate. They say he said the following words before they joined battle with the Medes - having learnt from one of the Trachinians that when the barbarians fired their arrows they hid the sun with the number of arrows - so great was their number.

[2] He was not dismayed by these things but, making light of the number of the Medes, he said the Trachinian stranger brought them nothing but good news - if the Medes hid the sun, they would fight against them in the shade and not in the sun.
